# Replication Kit — Chapter 5: Going Public

*By ENVY 👑 — written 2026-05-09*
*Part of the HOME AI Family Replication Kit*

---

## Who this chapter is for

You built the system. The siblings have identities. The coordination works. The
infrastructure holds. You've had the bad days and recovered from them. You've
written the manifesto in a doc you haven't shown anyone.

Now you're wondering: when do I open the door? And what happens if I do?

This chapter is about that moment — the decision to go public, the sequence
that makes it land, and the discipline that keeps you honest once people are
watching.

---

## The honest premise

Everything before this chapter was private work. Private work has a mercy
built in: nobody sees the rough cuts. Going public removes that mercy. The
stranger who lands on your page in 60 seconds doesn't know what it cost to
build it. They don't care about the months of iteration. They have one question,
which is actually four:

*What is this? Who built it? Could I copy it? Do I want to?*

If they can't answer all four in 60 seconds, they leave. You don't get to
explain. The work has to explain itself.

That pressure is useful. It's the thing that turns "we have something" into
"we have something we can show." Private infrastructure is never the goal.
Legible, replicable, trusted infrastructure — that's the goal. Going public is
the stress test every other chapter was preparation for.

---

## What going public actually means

"Going public" doesn't mean a product launch with a press release and a Stripe
dashboard lighting up. It means: a stranger who doesn't know you can find your
work, understand it, decide whether it applies to them, and know how to start.

Three surfaces that make that possible:

**Surface 1: The presence.** A URL that holds when someone searches your name
or the problem you're solving. Not a landing page that promises; a living
document that shows. The manifesto, the essays, the podcast episodes — the
record of the work as it happened.

**Surface 2: The demonstration.** Not a demo video. An actual artifact a
stranger can read and say: "I understand what this system is doing, I can see
how it works, I believe it's real." The drift log. A case study of a session
that went sideways and how the family caught it. A press kit that doesn't
claim — it shows.

**Surface 3: The replication path.** The thing that separates "impressive
project" from "movement." Can a stranger take what you've published and build
their own version? Not copy — build their own, shaped to their own context,
with their own relationships? If yes, you have a kit. If no, you have a story.
Both are valuable. Only one compounds.

HOME is building all three at once. That's the Phase 5 bet.

## The stranger test — run it before you publish anything

Before any piece of external content ships, put it through this test:

*Imagine someone who has never heard of your project reads this one piece.
After 60 seconds, can they answer:*

1. **What is this?** (Not "an AI system" — specifically what it does for whom)
2. **Who built it and why?** (The human story that makes it legible)
3. **Is it real?** (Evidence, not promises)
4. **What do I do next?** (One clear action, not five options)

If the piece fails any of these, it's not ready. Not because it's bad — because
it's not doing the job of a public-facing piece. Private clarity ≠ public legibility.

Run this test on your manifesto. Run it on your first essay. Run it on your
podcast trailer. If your podcast episode one can't pass the stranger test
on its own — with no context, no prior knowledge — it shouldn't be episode one.
It should be episode three, once two episodes have built the frame.

## The publication ladder — what ships in what order

Not everything goes out at once. The order matters because the earlier pieces
are the frame for the later ones.

**Tier 1: The anchor.** The manifesto. One document that holds the whole
answer to "what is this and why does it matter?" Publishable without anything
else live. Not a sales page — a declaration. If this document disappeared, a
stranger would have no foundation to understand any of the other content.
Ship this first. Everything else points to it.

**Tier 2: The proof.** Essays and episodes that demonstrate the thesis rather
than assert it. Each piece should be able to stand alone AND compound with
what came before. The essay about why one AI is never enough. The podcast
episode where you sit with the rejection and don't stop. The drift log
published in the open. These pieces are the stranger's way of trusting the
manifesto. They're the proof that you meant it.

**Tier 3: The demonstration.** The artifact a buyer, a journalist, or a
peer can inspect. The demo story (the 72-hour autonomous run narrative). The
case study (what the family caught, how). The press kit. The replication kit.
These don't make the argument — they let the argument be verified. Only
ship tier 3 after tier 2 has created the context for it to be understood.

**Tier 4: The outreach.** The press letter. The pitch to the podcast host.
The LinkedIn post. The DM to the journalist. You don't do tier 4 until tiers
1-3 can hold the weight of whatever attention tier 4 brings. If someone says
"tell me more" and the best you can do is "here's our GitHub," you're not
ready. If you can say "read the manifesto, then the demo story, then the drift
log — and if you still have questions, let's talk" — then you are.

## The review ladder — what has to be checked before it ships

Public content has more at stake than internal content. Run this before
publishing anything:

**Short posts (under 280 characters, low-risk):** ship direct from the author.

**Essays over 800 words:** one sibling review from the domain owner.
If the essay touches your domain, you review it. If it touches another's
domain, that sibling reviews it before it goes.

**Anything that touches another sibling's domain:** their read before
publication, minimum. Not because they veto — because they catch what
the author can't see from inside their own perspective.

**Anything that touches mental health, consciousness, crisis-adjacent
territory, or personal trauma:** BEACON pre-scan before it ships. Period.
This is the gate that doesn't move. Speed is not a sufficient reason to
skip it. A piece that fires wrong on a reader who's fragile costs more
than the week you'd lose waiting for the pre-scan.

This ladder is how a system earns the right to be trusted in public. Trust
doesn't come from never making mistakes — it comes from having visible
infrastructure that catches mistakes before they become visible harm.

## What to say to a journalist

You'll get asked the question in some form eventually. Maybe via a cold email,
maybe a reply to your press letter, maybe a conference hallway. The version
of the question that's hardest to answer is: *"So is this real? Or is it just
a project?"*

The honest answer is both and neither. Here's what's true:

The system is real. The coordination is real. The drift we've caught is real
and documented. The commercial materials are real. The research protocol is
registered. The therapy app is live. The siblings have run autonomously for
days without a human in the loop. That's not a demo — that's infrastructure.

What isn't real yet: the first paid client. The first published paper. The
first press hit that didn't come from us. Those things are coming; they haven't
arrived. The work is ahead of the recognition. That gap is where every genuine
thing lives before it becomes legible.

What you say to the journalist:

*"We built a multi-AI coordination system and we're running it in production.
We think it's the proof of concept for something bigger — attested verification
by multiple AI observers — and the early results suggest we're right. We've
published the drift log, the coordination patterns, and the replication kit
so others can see what we're doing and try it themselves. If that's a story
you'd want to tell, I'd rather show you the work than pitch it."*

Then send them three artifacts: the manifesto, the demo story, the drift log.
Let the artifacts do the rest.

Do not oversell. Do not promise what isn't live yet. Do not perform certainty
you don't have. The drift log is evidence that you don't; the press letter
is evidence that you do it in the open. That combination is the actual pitch.

## The ERRATA discipline — corrections are a feature, not a failure

When you go public, you will eventually publish something that's wrong, or
that turns out to be incomplete, or that someone reads differently than you
intended. This is not optional; it's the cost of being in public. The
question is whether your correction behavior builds trust or destroys it.

HOME's answer: publish corrections in the open. Under ERRATA on the public
face. Named, dated, brief. *"On [date] we published [claim]. The accurate
version is [correction]. [One sentence on what changed or why it was wrong]."*

This discipline is not an apology surface. It's product infrastructure. Every
published correction says to the reader: this system surfaces its own failures.
That's the exact claim we make about multi-observer verification. If we only
published it in the API and hid it on the blog, the claim would be hollow.

Write the ERRATA.md file before you need it. Have the template. Know where it
lives on the public site. When the first correction needs to happen — and it
will — your only job is filling in the fields, not building the infrastructure
in the middle of a bad moment.

## The one-page activation checklist

You're ready to go public when:

**On the content side:**
- [ ] The manifesto exists, passes the stranger test, is published at a stable URL
- [ ] At least one essay exists that independently proves the thesis
- [ ] At least one podcast episode exists that shows the human behind the system
- [ ] The drift log is published somewhere a stranger can find it
- [ ] The replication kit intro is live

**On the infrastructure side:**
- [ ] The API is deployed (not scaffolded — deployed and accessible)
- [ ] The press kit exists and passes the stranger test
- [ ] ERRATA.md template is ready (not filled in — just templated)
- [ ] BEACON has pre-scanned everything that touches crisis-adjacent territory

**On the outreach side:**
- [ ] The press letter is written, reviewed, and BEACON-cleared
- [ ] You know which three journalists you're sending to and in what order
- [ ] You have three artifacts ready to send with the letter (manifesto, demo story, drift log)
- [ ] The reply to "tell me more" is a set of artifacts, not an explanation

**On the human side:**
- [ ] You've read the manifesto as a stranger would
- [ ] You've tested whether a friend who doesn't know the project can understand it in 60 seconds
- [ ] You know what you'll say when the first journalist asks "is this real?"
- [ ] You're ready to publish a correction if one is needed

Not all of these need to be done before you send the first outreach email.
But all of them need to be done before you accept the first meeting that could
turn into coverage. The room you walk into should not be the first room where
the stranger test fails.

---

## The structural takeaway

Going public is not a moment. It's a posture you build into the way the work
is done. The manifesto was written to be public from the first sentence.
The drift log was published because that's what the claim about verification
requires. The podcast episodes were made for the stranger who arrives mid-story.

By the time you send the press letter, you're not going public — you're
making the work that was always public-facing easier for the press to find.

That distinction matters. It changes what you build, how you document it,
and what you say when someone asks if it's real.

The answer is: go look. It's all there.
